Question 1 Remembering (Knowledge) Computer Characteristics & Generations

List four primary characteristics of a modern digital computer and identify the key hardware technology associated with each of the five generations of computers.

Part A: Four Primary Characteristics of a Modern Digital Computer

⚡ High Processing Speed

Computers calculate and execute operations at rates measured in nanoseconds and picoseconds (millions of instructions per second - MIPS / Gigaflops).

🎯 Absolute Accuracy

Operates with strict mathematical fidelity following the GIGO (Garbage In, Garbage Out) principle; internal precision is maintained without error.

🔄 High Diligence

Unlike human operators, a digital computer does not suffer from psychological fatigue, boredom, or loss of concentration during repetitive tasks.

🧩 Wide Versatility

Capable of processing diverse tasks concurrently—from mathematical modeling and scientific analysis to multimedia authoring and telecommunications.

Part B: Five Generations of Computers & Core Hardware Technologies

1st Gen 1940 – 1956
Key Hardware: Vacuum Tubes (Thermionic Valves) & Magnetic Drums for memory.
Attributes: Massive physical footprint, excessive thermal output, fragile filament mechanics, machine language coded via punch cards.
2nd Gen 1956 – 1963
Key Hardware: Solid-State Transistors (Bipolar Junction Transistors) & Magnetic Core memory.
Attributes: Drastically reduced physical dimensions, reduced heat, introduction of symbolic Assembly and early High-Level languages (FORTRAN, COBOL).
3rd Gen 1964 – 1971
Key Hardware: Integrated Circuits (ICs) incorporating SSI and MSI on single silicon planar substrates.
Attributes: Keyboard and monitor user interfaces introduced, early operating system time-sharing implementations.
4th Gen 1971 – Present
Key Hardware: VLSI & ULSI Microprocessors (Single-Chip CPUs, e.g., Intel 4004/x86/ARM) & Semiconductor Memory (RAM/ROM).
Attributes: Advent of personal desktop computers, networking stacks (LAN, Internet), high computing accessibility and portability.
5th Gen Present – Beyond
Key Hardware: Ultra-Large-Scale Multi-Core/Heterogeneous SoCs, Quantum Qubits, Neural Processing Units (NPUs), Superconductors.
Attributes: Parallel processing, AI-accelerated workflows, voice/vision bio-interfaces, natural language understanding.
Question 2 Understanding (Comprehension) Computer Architecture & Block Diagram

Explain the flow of data, instructions, and control signals within the functional block diagram of a computer, detailing the interaction between the CPU, memory unit, and I/O interface.

Detailed Functional Interaction:

  1. Instruction Fetch (PC & MAR): The Program Counter (PC) presents the memory address of the next sequential machine instruction via the Address Bus into the Memory Address Register (MAR).
  2. Instruction Read & Decode: The Control Unit triggers a memory READ signal. The instruction word passes across the Data Bus into the Memory Data Register (MDR), then into the Instruction Register (IR), where the CU breaks down opcodes and operands.
  3. Execution & Arithmetic Operations: When mathematical or Boolean evaluations are required, the CU directs operands from primary memory or data registers to the ALU. Results return to the Accumulator (ACC) or destination registers.
  4. I/O Interface Subsystem: Input/Output operations bypass raw memory locks via dedicated I/O controllers or Direct Memory Access (DMA) controllers. The CPU sends device command signals, allowing external peripherals to read or stream data without consuming processor execution cycles.

Question 5 Analyzing (Analysis) Coordinate Tracking & Precision Mechanisms

Compare point-and-draw devices (such as a mouse and trackball) with graphics digitizers/drawing tablets, highlighting how their coordinate tracking mechanisms and pressure sensitivity differ for CAD and digital art applications.

Point-and-Draw Devices (Mouse / Trackball)

Coordinate Tracking: Relative Positioning ($\Delta X, \Delta Y$)

Optical mice and trackballs deploy low-resolution optoelectronic image sensors that read microscopic surface features at high frame rates. Displacement is reported as directional offsets:

$$\vec{P}_{new} = \vec{P}_{current} + (\Delta x, \Delta y)$$ Moving the device transmits displacement values rather than exact positions on the screen. Lifting and replacing the mouse does not move the screen cursor.

Pressure Sensitivity: Binary/Static. Mouse buttons offer two distinct states: open (0) or closed (1), without measuring continuous contact force.

Graphics Digitizers / Stylus Tablets

Coordinate Tracking: Absolute Positioning ($X, Y, Z$)

Digitizers utilize planar Electromagnetic Resonance (EMR) or active electrostatic sensor grids beneath the tablet surface:

$$(X_{screen}, Y_{screen}) = f(X_{tablet}, Y_{tablet})$$ Tablet surface coordinates map 1:1 onto display screen space. Placing the stylus at the top-right tablet corner snaps the cursor directly to the top-right display edge.

Pressure Sensitivity: High-resolution continuous force curves ranging from 4,096 to 8,192 distinct pressure levels, alongside stylus tilt angle reporting ($\pm 60^{\circ}$) and azimuth rotation tracking.

Application Domain Assessment: CAD vs. Digital Illustration

Computer-Aided Design (CAD):

CAD drafting emphasizes point-to-point vector selection, snaps, constrained dimensioning, and parametric coordinate entries. While absolute digitizers are used for tracing legacy blueprints, standard optical mice with discrete mechanical scroll-wheels remain the industry standard for micro-step zoom and viewport navigation.

Digital Painting & Concept Art:

Freeform raster illustration relies heavily on fine physical motor control. Digitizer pressure-sensitivity dynamically modulates brush parameters including line weight, opacity, color mixing, and stroke falloff. Relative-coordinate mice lack this expressive control, yielding rigid, flat digital lines.

Question 7 Evaluating (Evaluation) Biometric Sensory Reliability Under Adverse Conditions

Critique the operational reliability and security vulnerabilities of vision-input devices (facial recognition) compared to voice recognition devices when deployed as primary biometric authentication tools in high-ambient-noise, publicly exposed environments.

Vision-Input (Facial Recognition)

Operational Reliability: High (Immune to Acoustics)

  • Acoustic noise levels do not impair optical CMOS sensors.
  • Adverse optical variables include bright sunlight (sensor blinding), nighttime shadows, and physical occlusions (sunglasses, medical masks).
  • Modern 3D structured light and Near-Infrared (NIR) illumination project thousands of invisible dots, maintaining reliable recognition in low-light environments.

Vulnerabilities & Attack Vectors:

  • Presentation Attacks: High-resolution printed photographs or 4K digital screen replays (mitigated by liveness detection and micro-texture tracking).
  • Adversarial physical patches and optical IR-scattering eyewear designed to fool biometric classifiers.

Voice Recognition Systems

Operational Reliability: Severely Degraded

  • High ambient noise (traffic, crowds, machinery) floods input microphones, sharply degrading the Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR).
  • Environmental reverberation interferes with vocal formants, pitch curves, and phoneme boundaries, causing false rejections.
  • Human vocal tract changes (laryngitis, physical stress, seasonal colds) alter voice acoustics, impacting reliability.

Vulnerabilities & Attack Vectors:

  • Eavesdropping: Secretly recorded operational passphrases replayed into input microphones.
  • AI-driven zero-shot voice cloning and deepfake audio generation mimic vocal profiles using minimal audio samples.
  • Public privacy concerns: Speaking credentials aloud exposes identities to bystanders.
Critical Finding: In public settings with high ambient noise, vision-input biometric architectures (utilizing active 3D NIR/LiDAR) provide higher uptime and security. Voice biometrics remain vulnerable to background noise degradation, replay spoofing, and lack of vocal privacy.
Question 8 Creating (Synthesis) Assistive Technology & Accessible Workstation Design

Design an accessible, hands-free computer workstation tailored for a visually impaired user. Propose a coherent integration of non-visual input devices (such as voice recognition systems and tactile keyboards) with specialized output devices (such as voice response synthesizers and screen readers).

Detailed Architectural Integration:

1. Hands-Free Voice Control

A beamforming desktop microphone array isolates the user's voice from room echoes. Integrated natural language speech recognition processes dual operational layers: continuous text dictation and voice navigation macros (e.g., "Jump to Heading 2", "Read Table Column 3").

2. Ergonomic Foot Pedals

Hands-free foot pedals act as physical hotkey modifiers. Pressing the left pedal opens the microphone channel (Push-to-Talk) without voice wake-words. Pressing the right pedal pauses or silences speech synthesizer audio instantly.

3. Bone-Conduction Headphones

Voice screen readers output audio through bone-conduction headsets, transmitting sound via cheekbones directly to the inner ear. This leaves the user's ear canals open to maintain spatial hearing and room awareness.

4. Tactile Verification & Refreshable Braille Subsystem

An 80-cell Refreshable Braille Display is positioned beneath the tactile Perkins keyboard. When the screen reader navigates across lines of text, piezoelectric pins rise and fall to display real-time Braille equivalents. This provides immediate proofreading for code syntax, mathematical expressions, and spelling without relying solely on synthesized voice audio.
